Closed via #121 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/provenance` (14 tests). No artifact runs in a preview unless its provenance proves who built it, from what, and that the proof is authentic — an unattested artifact is an artifact nobody can account for, not a deployable artifact with missing paperwork, so verification is a gate rather than a label. Every property is checked in fail-safe order: presence, then signature, then builder authorization, then artifact/source match, then freshness. Signature precedes authorization because an unauthorized signer with a valid signature needs investigation while a forged one needs rebuilding; identity precedes freshness because a stale attestation from the right builder needs a rebuild while a fresh one from the wrong builder needs investigation. Each missing identity field — artifact, repository, source, builder — is a different way to smuggle an unaccounted artifact past the gate, so each is refused. A valid signature from an unauthorized builder is refused distinctly. Attestations older than 30 days are refused: a build from six months ago attests nothing about today's toolchain, whose builder, inputs, and compromises may all have changed. Builders cannot mint anonymous attestations, an empty key would attest anything, and a builder-registry outage is a fault rather than a denial. Descriptions never carry signatures. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** the preview cloud account, the KMS boundaries, and the evidence signing algorithm/trust root and key-rotation decisions.
- [x] G0.5 **[DONE]** Create least-privilege identities for controller, build, allocator, runner, runtime, experiment, janitor and signer. Closed via #120 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/roles` (16 tests). Least privilege is a **matrix, not a motto**: 8 roles × 15 actions, every cell decided, no implicit defaults — a matrix with implicit defaults is one nobody can review. The load-bearing denials are written as data: build cannot provision, sign, or touch secrets or the ledger (the G0 gate, pinned as a test); runners mutate and destroy but cannot sign, since a runner that could sign its own evidence could fake a pass; the runtime serves and reads its scoped credential but writes nothing, since a workload that could write evidence could qualify itself; the janitor retries teardown and quarantines but cannot provision, since it could otherwise manufacture what it discovers; the signer signs and reads, nothing else, since broader rights make it a confused deputy; the controller decides but never provisions, mutates, or destroys, since that would bypass the ledger and its fencing; experiments inject faults but cannot rebuild, since a fault that rebuilds is an outage with a costume on; allocators provision but never destroy, since destruction goes through verified cleanup. Creators write credentials, consumers only read — a role doing both could rotate a credential out from under its own workload unnoticed. Unknown roles and unknown actions **deny**: a typo must fail closed, not open a hole shaped exactly like the typo. Every action is held by someone (no dead policy — the tests caught two) and denied to someone (no boundary-less action), and no role does everything (no superuser, or every other row is decorative). Each decision carries a reason, because a denial nobody understands becomes a ticket for broader rights, and broader rights to stop the tickets is how least privilege dies. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** binding these identities to cloud IAM roles and the contract fixtures in G0.6.

- [x] G1.3 **[DONE]** Add observe-before-retry for uncertain native operations and per-environment mutation serialization. **Covers G-F06, G-F07.** Evidence: `internal/actions` (uncertain vs failed semantics, verified-absent returns to planned), `internal/postgres` (epoch fences takeover), `internal/reconcile` (observation ordered before work and never starved), `internal/statelock` (17), migration 0001 — 128 tests. Mutation is serialised by a **native** provider-side lock, never a platform-database lock, because "database epochs fence ledger updates, not AWS/Kubernetes/Terraform side effects by themselves". A runner reference is mandatory, since without one a replacement could never confirm termination. A replacement is refused while the previous runner's operation is in flight, and is refused again while its state is unknown — treating unknown as idle is how two mutators end up on one environment. Exclusivity is re-verified before destructive steps and any failure to verify reads as lost, since unverified exclusivity is the same danger as none. Twelve concurrent runners admit exactly one.
- [x] G1.4 **[DONE]** Implement quotas/TTL reservations and extension/destroy authorization. **Covers G-F06.** Evidence: `internal/quota` (23), `internal/admission` (17), `internal/ttlwatch` (15), migration 0001 `capacity_reservations` — 61 tests. Admission reserves repository/actor/fleet concurrency, CPU/storage ceilings and monetary allowance **transactionally across all four scopes at once** — validate-then-insert is the race SPEC.md warns about, so every scope is checked before any is consumed and a refusal leaves nothing held. Twenty-four concurrent controllers against one slot admit exactly one; a concurrent-admission test proves the fleet never oversubscribes. Partial reservations cannot occur, because they leak capacity no environment accounts for. A reservation must name its environment, and a scope with no grant is refused rather than treated as unlimited. Expired windows grant nothing. Release is idempotent, because a retried cleanup must not credit a slot twice and invent capacity nobody paid for. TTL defaults to 24h and clamps to the 72h maximum rather than refusing, since a caller asking for too long should get the policy maximum but never the extra time. Extension requires all three of an authorized actor, remaining fleet/cost allowance, and an audit record naming an authorised capacity revision — each a distinct failure, because an audit record without an allowance check is just a log line, and exceeding the extension cap directs an operator to a revision rather than a retry loop. Destroy requires an identified actor and a stated reason, so a customer has something to appeal with. Administrative grace must carry an explicit cap, an expiry and a reason, since unbounded grace is indistinguishable from an environment that will never be reclaimed. The watchdog is a separate component with its own store — `ttlwatch.New` refuses a nil store, because a stuck controller must never be the thing deciding an environment lives forever. Expiry does not wait for a gate, marks absent before releasing capacity so a slot is never freed while an environment still occupies it, and reports a failed release separately from a failed expiry because a leaked slot is retryable while a leaked environment is not. Exhaustion surrenders capacity in the spec's order — stop admission, stop live providers, stop experiments, drain, destroy — and a policy reaching destroy before stop-admission is refused, since that would empty and refill the fleet repeatedly, burning money instead of conserving it.
- [x] G1.5 **[DONE]** Implement resource/native-ID ledger, revocation/removal and verified cleanup. **Covers G-F06.** Evidence: `internal/resource` (21), `internal/cleanup` (19), migration 0001 `resource_allocations` + `resource_observations` — 44 tests. The ledger is the **only** source of delete authority, and both halves of that are enforced as tests rather than comments: `NewAuthorityIssuer` refuses a nil ledger, because without it every authority would be unbacked, and refuses a nil denylist, because an empty one would silently permit deleting a foundation resource. Delete authority is a distinct type that must be obtained through verification, so a delete cannot be issued from something that merely looks like an allocation, and it carries the provider reference that was verified rather than re-reading the ledger at delete time. Shared resources are checked twice, independently: against the ledger's own `is_shared` flag *and* against the foundation denylist, because either signal alone can be wrong. The denylist checks **every** value in the opaque provider ref, so a provider that names its native ID differently cannot bypass it. `ResolveByName` exists only to refuse: "a destructive action never follows user-supplied names", and there is no safe version of the operation. An allocation with no provider reference is rejected, because it could never be observed and would guarantee an unverifiable leak. An unknown resource is refused — nobody can prove it is safe to delete. Generation fences the destroy. Revocation requires an identified actor and comes strictly before destruction, since credentials outlive the data they grant access to; when revocation fails, nothing is destroyed. Revocation is idempotent and irreversible. The verified-deleted row is a **tombstone**: re-recording it is refused, so a stale webhook cannot recreate a removed allocation, and absence may never be recorded while the resource still exists. Cleanup runs in the spec's order — revoke, then destroy in dependency-safe order (data before the bindings that reach it), then verify each resource immediately rather than the whole environment at the end. **A destroy call that returned successfully is not proof:** the provider can accept a call and not apply it, so a resource that still exists stays `cleanup_verifying` and is never reported destroyed, and an uncertain destroy is verified on the next pass rather than retried, because re-destroying a resource that already succeeded is how a shared resource gets destroyed twice. Verification requires an installed observer — absence is never assumed. Verification backoff is bounded. One stuck resource does not block reclamation of the others. Skips are classified by type, not by substring-matching error text, so whether to page an operator does not depend on prose staying as written; a shared resource is an intentional exclusion and does not page, while an unremovable resource preserves state and pages. Operator output never carries a credential location, and allocations lacking ownership tags are flagged because post-restore reconciliation depends on them.
- [x] G1.6 **[DONE]** Add independent janitor for expired/missing-ledger resource recovery. Evidence: `internal/janitor` — 17 tests. The janitor has two jobs from SPEC.md: retry failed teardown, and observe owner-tagged native resources to reconcile state after a crash. Its defining property is **what it refuses to do**: it discovers resources that exist in the provider with no ledger row, and it quarantines rather than deletes them. An unowned resource is the one case where "delete what isn't ours" is most likely, because nobody can prove whose it is — it may be another preview's, a customer's own, or the platform's foundation — so a person decides. Quarantine is the *only* outcome of that branch, and a test asserts nothing is ever deleted on it. Ownership is checked against the tag key, the tag value must be non-empty (an empty tag on a resource the janitor did not create is worse than none, because it looks like an answer), **and** the named environment must actually have the native id in its ledger: a tag without provenance is an ownership claim with nothing behind it, and is treated exactly like an untagged resource. A resource recorded as `verified_deleted` that the provider still reports is the resurrection case, and it is quarantined rather than cleaned — the tombstone was correct when written, so something recreated it and that disagreement is not something to tidy away quietly. A **discovery failure is never a clean pass**, because a janitor that could not look is indistinguishable from one that did not look, and a retried-but-still-pending teardown keeps the pass from reading as clean because a pending teardown is a leak in progress. Sighting history persists across passes so a single tag-propagation lag does not raise an incident, while a genuinely unowned resource is caught after a configurable number of consecutive sightings; an un-actionable incident is one people learn to ignore. Becoming tracked clears a resource's suspicion. The janitor requires provider discovery, because its own ledger could only ever confirm what it already believed and could never find a leak.
- [x] G1 gate **[DONE]**: duplicate/reorder/crash/timeout scenarios cannot leak unowned resources or resurrect destroyed IDs. Evidence: `test/gate` — independent reference model + 15 tests over `test/fake`, `internal/{resource,cleanup,janitor}`. **The gate found a real bug.** Building it surfaced that `MemoryLedger.Record` accepted an out-of-order event carrying an **older generation** and overwrote the record — which would have let a delayed webhook authorize cleanup of something a later generation had replaced. `Record` now fences generation on write, refuses a downgrade, still accepts forward progress, and has a regression test in `internal/resource`. Two invariants are checked against an independently-written model: no resource exists that is neither expected nor quarantined, and no tombstoned key reappears. Coverage: a duplicate delivery is refused by the ledger's uniqueness constraint and 16 concurrent duplicates are all refused; a reordered older-generation event is refused and the generation does not move; a crash between provider create and ledger write leaves an orphan that the janitor **quarantines and does not delete**; a tombstoned allocation cannot be recreated by a late delivery; a create that times out after the native call applied is resolved by observation with **no recreation**; and a permission failure is not absence. The scenarios run both in isolation and as **one continuous history**, because isolated tests prove each property separately and cannot prove the properties hold together — the crash must not undermine the duplicate rule, the timeout must not undermine the tombstone. A negative control breaks each invariant deliberately and requires the checker to notice, because a gate that cannot fail is not a gate. All violations are reported at once rather than stopping at the first. The model reads provider state directly rather than trusting the platform's own ledger, since a ledger claiming absence while the provider still holds the resource is exactly the disagreement being looked for. **Scope limit, as the checklist requires:** this gate is satisfied against the faked reference model only. Deployed native-resource proof is the G2 gate and is **not** claimed here.

## G2 — Cloud allocation and isolation

- [x] G2.1 **[DONE]** Provision foundation Terraform and qualified per-environment module/state locks. Closed via #114 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/foundation` (22 tests), `infrastructure/foundation` + `infrastructure/environment`, and a new CI job running `terraform fmt -check` and `validate` so unverifiable HCL cannot ship. The testable core is **qualification**, because SPEC.md separates a once-deployed platform foundation from a per-preview disposable module, and mixing them is how a preview teardown destroys what every other preview stands on. Consuming a foundation output is gated on qualification evidence, not on the value existing: an **unqualified output is refused, and that is the package's central property**, because an unqualified network does not error when used — it simply isolates badly, so a *missing* output fails visibly at wiring time while an *unqualified* one is used and the problem surfaces later as a preview that can reach what it should not. Presence is not qualification: a failed check refuses, and the refusal names which check so it is diagnosable. **An absent check is not a passed check** — the likeliest bug is forgetting to run a check rather than a check failing, so evidence that simply omits a required check is refused. Qualification by nobody is not qualification, lapsed evidence is refused, and an empty value is refused. A foundation output cannot be published as environment-owned, so a mislabelled output is never dependable rather than relying on every consumer to check. **Destroy scope is derived by exclusion**: everything platform-owned is excluded, so a newly added foundation output is excluded by default. An allowlist would make every new output destroyable until someone noticed. A resource that is neither platform-owned nor among the environment's recorded native ids is refused, the same reasoning the resource ledger applies to an unknown resource. State location is derived from the environment id alone — a caller-chosen prefix would be a traversal primitive against the platform's own state bucket, so ids and bucket names containing separators are refused even though the id is platform-issued; two environments can never share a prefix or a lock. In HCL the same properties are structural: the KMS key's deletion is denied, the preview role's deny against platform-owned resources uses a wildcard principal that no narrower grant can widen, IAM and KMS mutation is denied outright, the state bucket has no unencrypted-write path, and the per-environment module asserts its own state key shape rather than trusting the caller. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** `plan`/`apply` against a real account and deployed verification of the isolation properties. No credentials and no Terraform binary are available in this environment, so `fmt`/`validate` run in CI and nothing beyond that is asserted.

- [x] G2.3 **[DONE]** Install namespace/network/egress/resource/pod policies before candidate pods. Closed via #115 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/policygate` (15 tests). The testable core is **ordering as a safety property**: a pod that starts before its network policy exists has a window in which it can reach anything, and that window is exactly when an attacker-controlled image would use it. So pod admission depends on **verified policy presence, not on policies having been requested** — requesting and having are different states, and only one of them constrains anything. **There is no partial readiness**: a namespace with a quota but no default-deny is a namespace whose pods can reach anything, and calling that "mostly ready" would admit them. Without default-deny every allow rule is only a maximum rather than a default. The required set is **fixed, not per-preview configurable**, because a preview that could opt out of its default-deny would. An observation failure is a fault, not a verdict — an unreadable cluster must refuse pods, not admit them, and the cause is preserved rather than wrapped. **Drift is distinct from never-ready**: a policy that was present and is now gone means something removed a boundary, which needs a person rather than a retry; retrying through removed-boundary drift would keep pods running unconstrained while waiting for the policy to come back. But readiness outside the trust window is just a re-check, since treating stale memory as evidence of removal would page on every slow re-verification. Readiness expires after 5 minutes: re-verified, not remembered. Every admission check observes rather than caches, so concurrent checks cannot disagree. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** wiring the gate into pod admission against a real cluster and deployed proof. - [x] G2.4 **[DONE]** Build isolated preview migration/seed and signed immutable role deployment. Closed via #116 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/seed` (21 tests). The testable core is **binding at execution, not just at scheduling**: a seed run is checked against the database it arrived at, because scheduling and execution can disagree — a queued seed from a destroyed environment must not run against whatever the scheduler now points at. A seed at the wrong database is refused and its body never executes. **Ordering is enforced before idempotency**: a migration builds on the schema its predecessor left, so the predecessor must have completed; only a correctly placed, correctly ordered seed may be skipped as already done. A rerun of the same digest is a no-op reported distinctly, so a caller can tell "did work" from "nothing to do"; a *different* digest under the same id is a new migration and runs. **A failed seed is never recorded**: marking a half-applied migration done would make the next run skip the repair. Seeds are namespaced per environment, so one preview's migrations cannot satisfy another's ordering. Whole runs are serialized under one lock — check-execute-record is only idempotent if nothing interleaves, and twelve concurrent reruns execute exactly once. Roles are **content-addressed and pinned**: the digest covers sorted statements plus the environment scope, so a role minted for one preview cannot be replayed into another, and reordering statements is not a mutation. The pin is signed; an unsigned role, a widened role carrying an old signature, and a live role that drifted from its pin are all refused. A change is a new digest, never an edit — the old definition stops verifying the moment it is replaced. Descriptions never carry signatures, since a signature in a log is replayable in the wrong place. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** execution against a real database, KMS-backed signing instead of the HMAC development keeper, and deployed proof. Neither is available in this environment.
- [x] G2.5 **[DONE]** Implement authenticated preview URL and identity/config readiness checks. Closed via #117 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/previewaccess` (19 tests). A preview URL is a **capability**: whoever holds it can see the preview, so every URL carries a signed token bound to one environment, one principal, and one purpose, with a short 15-minute expiry. A token for env-A does not open env-B; a view token does not authorize interaction; an expired token is refused rather than honoured. **No URL is issued into an unready environment**: issuance requires fresh authentication, authorization for that environment and purpose, and passing identity + config-currency checks — issuing first and checking later would hand out a capability and then try to take it back, which fails once the URL is copied. Stale authentication cannot mint, and an anonymous caller cannot mint. Serve-time verification checks shape, signature, expiry, environment binding, purpose, and revocation, in an order that fails safe. A token issued in the future opens nothing, whether the cause is clock skew or games. **An unknowable revocation answer refuses**: the safe answer to "was this revoked?" when the answer cannot be read is to refuse. Nonces are unique per issuance even when the clock does not advance, so revoking one token revokes exactly one token — its sibling keeps working. Parsing and verifying stay separate, because a malformed token is a client bug while a well-formed token with a bad signature is an attack, and collapsing them slows incident response. An authorizer outage is a fault, not a denial — wrapping it as unauthorized would hide the outage. Descriptions never carry signatures, since claims plus signature in a log is a replay kit. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** gateway enforcement, URL delivery with DNS/certificates (G2.9), KMS-backed signing, and deployed proof. None is available in this environment.

- [x] G2.9 **[DONE]** Own preview URL delivery: wildcard DNS, certificate issuance/renewal/revocation, the ACME/API identity, HSTS and origin binding. Closed via #119 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/certlifecycle` (19 tests). DNS and ACME need the network, so what is owned here is the lifecycle state machine, the renewal discipline, revocation on environment end, and origin binding at serve time. **Renew before expiry, not after**: a certificate's end date is known from issuance, so there is never a reason to discover expiry by serving one — renewal starts with a third of lifetime left, giving a 90-day certificate 30 days for failed attempts with backoff. Renewal failure keeps the old certificate serving **up to its expiry, never past it**: taking a preview offline over an ACME outage would turn every such outage into downtime, while serving past expiry trains clients to accept expired certificates. The old expiry is the hard bound either way, and a renewal failure surfaces distinctly from a serving failure so an operator can tell them apart. A revoked certificate is **replaced, never renewed**, and an expired one likewise — renewal extends a chain of trust, and a chain that ended is not extended. **Revoke at teardown, not after**: a torn-down preview whose certificate still validates is a trusted identity for something that no longer exists; revocation needs a reason, is idempotent so teardown retries are safe, and is a no-op for environments that never got far enough to need a certificate. **Origin binding is verified against the certificate's own record**: env-A's certificate never serves env-B even if routing is wrong, and per-environment lifecycles never touch each other. A second issuance while one is live is refused, because two trusted identities for one origin means revocation can only ever be partial. An issuer bug returning no serial or an already-ended certificate is refused rather than recorded — a record that can never serve is worse than no record, because it looks like an answer. HSTS requires a positive max-age **with** subdomains: a wildcard preview domain without includeSubDomains leaves every sibling preview on plaintext, and max-age zero disables the policy — both refused, not warned, since a warning in a log is not a boundary. Descriptions omit serials, which are enough to revoke with. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** wildcard DNS, ACME issuance against a real CA, and deployed proof. All three need the network. This is a launch-blocking operational dependency, not an implicit property of the gateway.

## G3 — Independent gates and reporting

- [x] G3.1 **[DONE]** Build platform-owned smoke/isolation/replay/search/load harnesses and mock receivers/providers. Closed via #123 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/harness` (20 tests). **Platform-owned is the load-bearing word**: a harness the candidate controls can be taught to pass, so scenarios, assertions, and verdicts all live here and the candidate only ever sees probes. Outcomes are pass/fail/**inconclusive**/canceled per SPEC §9, and inconclusive is not a softer fail — it means the harness itself could not tell, which needs a fixed harness rather than a fixed candidate. Unknown, partial, and no-traffic outcomes do not pass, and that rule is enforced by the outcome type rather than hoped for. The identity is checked before anything runs: a verdict bound to a partial identity would qualify code it never examined. Isolation acts in one target and requires a second to have observed nothing; a shared target standing in for both fails loudly, which is exactly a broken boundary. Replay requires identical outputs for identical inputs; load is bounded (default 100) with an SLO and an error tolerance, and a canceled load is inconclusive because a partial load run proves nothing. Mocks model the defects under test rather than the happy path: silent write loss (worse than failing loudly), nondeterminism, and every failure mode per operation. Two mock bugs were caught while writing the tests — a counter in the marker made every act unique so replay could never pass, and wall-clock nanos made nondeterminism invisible on coarse timers. Every run emits evidence carrying the full candidate identity, measurements, and elapsed time — the fields G3.2's binding verification will consume. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** driving real cluster targets and deployed proof.
- [x] G3.2 **[DONE]** Bind gate evidence to source/base/artifact/config/recipe/schema/policy/generation. Closed via #124 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/evidence` (14 tests). A gate result that is not bound to its candidate is an ornament — it says "pass" without saying pass *of what*, and an unbound pass is reusable: yesterday's pass for yesterday's code becomes today's qualification for today's code, which is how a candidate ships without ever being tested. Binding is **total across all eight dimensions**: source, base, artifact, config, recipe, schema, policy, generation. Seven of eight matching is not bound, because the missing digest is exactly the dimension along which the candidate changed. Checks run in fail-safe diagnostic order: outcome first (only a pass qualifies — fail, inconclusive, and canceled presented as qualification are refused outright, not reinterpreted or upgraded), completeness second (a result that does not say what it qualifies cannot qualify anything), signature third (only then is the record worth comparing), generation fourth (a result for an older generation is stale even if every digest matches — the G1 gate's fencing discipline applied to gate results with equal force), digests last with each difference named (the differing digest is the dimension the candidate changed along, which is what an operator needs to know). Expired evidence does not qualify. An unpinned expectation dimension accepts any value — pinning nothing means trusting everything there. Descriptions never carry signatures. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** wiring into the evidence store/dashboard and deployed proof.
- [x] G3.3 **[DONE]** Enforce observation completeness/sample confidence and inconclusive states. Closed via #125 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/confidence` (12 tests). A verdict is only as good as its observations: three passing probes prove three probes ran, not health. So a verdict requires **completeness** (enough observations covering every required dimension) and **confidence** (a pass rate whose lower bound clears the bar, not a point estimate above it) — anything short is inconclusive, and inconclusive never qualifies. The **lower bound is the whole point**: 3/3 has a point estimate of 100% and a 95% lower bound near 44%, consistent with a coin flip; accepting the point estimate would qualify candidates on evidence that proves nothing. The Wilson interval is used rather than the normal approximation precisely because the normal approximation collapses at the boundaries that matter — near-perfect pass rates with small samples, where it would report 100% ± 0% for 3/3 and bless the evidence that proves least. Checks run in fail-safe diagnostic order: coverage first (a verdict about an unobserved dimension is a verdict about nothing, which needs different fixing than low confidence), sample size second (thirty beats three, stated not felt), raw failure rate third (too many failures fails no matter what the bound says), confidence last. The interval math is pinned against independently known values (95/100 ≈ 0.8882, 3/3 ≈ 0.4385, 1/1 ≈ 0.2065 — a single passing probe proves almost nothing) plus monotonicity and clamping properties, so a regression in the math is caught rather than trusted. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** enforcing this inside gate evaluation against live harnesses and deployed proof.
- [x] G3.4 **[DONE]** Sign/redact/store evidence outside candidate write scope; authorized dashboard access. Closed via #126 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/evidencestore` (11 tests). Two separations, both structural. **Write scope**: evidence qualifies a candidate, so a candidate that can write evidence can qualify itself — the store accepts writes only from platform roles (controller, harness, janitor), never from workloads or candidate identities, and the check runs on **every write**, not once at setup. A store that trusted its callers would be a ledger with aspirations. **Redaction before persistence**: evidence routinely contains what it observed — URLs with tokens, bearer headers, secret URIs, PEM blocks, access keys — so every record is scrubbed for secret patterns before it is written; storing verbatim would turn the evidence store into a credential store with worse access controls. What cannot be scrubbed with confidence is **refused rather than stored hopefully**. Records are content-addressed with SHA-256 excluding the timestamp (a weak hash would let two records share an address — history rewritten by arithmetic), append-only, and generation-bound; a duplicate write resolves to the same record, and sixteen concurrent duplicate writes store exactly once. Reads are authorized per principal and scope with an empty scope reading nothing: a dashboard for one environment does not read another's, since evidence about a preview is information about its data. Benign content survives scrubbing byte-identical. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** a durable backend, dashboard wiring, and deployed proof.
- [x] G3.5 **[DONE]** Implement GitHub check/deployment reporting outbox and current-head verification. Closed via #127 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/reportoutbox` (11 tests). A status posted to the wrong commit is worse than no status — a green check on a stale SHA is how untested code gets merged — so every report is verified against the current head **before it is sent**, and a report for anything but the current head is **refused, not queued**, since queueing it would deliver a lie the moment the network recovers. The head is verified at queue time so a stale report never enters the outbox, **and re-verified at flush time** because the head may have moved while queued; a report that was current then and stale now is dropped — not sent, not retried — and the drop is reported so the caller can re-queue against the new head. Delivery sends with idempotency keys and retries without duplicating: a gateway timeout after the post still posts once, attempts are counted, and a failed delivery stays queued. Queueing deduplicates identical reports, since two identical statuses would flip a check run's history; state transitions (queued → in-progress → completed) are correctly three separate reports. Concurrent flushes claim reports under the lock into an in-flight set, so the second flush finds nothing pending — double delivery is impossible rather than merely unlikely, and a crashed flush cannot strand a claim because failures requeue. A completed check without a conclusion is refused, reports must name repository/context/SHA, and an unresolvable head surfaces rather than silently queueing into the unknown. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** the GitHub API sender and deployed proof.
- [x] G3.6 **[DONE]** Invalidate/cancel gates on updates/expiry/close and protect attestation integrity. Closed via #128 at merge by owner. Evidence: `internal/invalidation` (15 tests). A gate result qualifies a candidate at a moment, and three events end that moment: a source update (the code under test is no longer the code that would ship), an expiry (time moved, not code), and a PR close (nothing left to qualify). The response has **two halves, and both are required**: in-flight runs are canceled, because finishing would produce fresh verdicts about a dead question; completed passes are invalidated, because leaving them valid would let a future deployment cite them. Canceling without invalidating leaves loaded history; invalidating without canceling lets new verdicts land on a closed question. Half a response is not a response. Only passes are invalidated — a fail never qualified anything, so there is nothing to take away, and recording an invalidation for one would imply it once counted. Scope narrows per environment so one preview's update does not cancel its sibling's gates, while a PR close ends every preview's question at once. A verdict for a canceled run is refused on arrival: a slow harness must not outlive the invalidation that stopped it, and verdicts land exactly once. **Attestation integrity**: attestations are signed at seal time and verified at use time; an edited verdict (the classic fail-to-pass upgrade) is refused rather than re-examined, since re-examining a tampered record treats an attack as a data quality problem. Use requires both halves — a valid signature over an untracked run is a forgery with good cryptography, and a tracked run with a broken signature cannot be trusted. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** wiring into the gate runners and deployed proof.
- [ ] G3.7 **[WIP]** Install SLO instrumentation, metrics pipeline and alerting for the gate surfaces at this phase, not at G6.4, because G3–G5 evidence depends on it. G6.4 retains rotation, on-call ownership and exception policy. **Closes Q-09 telemetry-context storage posture.** Evidence: `internal/slo` (12 tests). An SLO without burn-rate alerting is a dashboard, not an objective: it reports after the month that the month was bad. Burn rate — consumption relative to the exactly-exhausting rate — turns "are we breaching?" into "will we breach, and how fast?", which is what paging depends on. **Two windows, because one window lies**: a short window alone pages on every transient, a long window alone pages after the budget is gone. Fast-burn pages sudden breakage; slow-burn tickets gradual decay — and the slow threshold sits strictly below the fast one, enforced at definition time. Judging on fewer than the minimum sample is **refused rather than guessed**: an alert from three events is noise with a pager attached, and a quiet verdict from three events is a clean bill of health nobody earned. Out-of-order telemetry is accepted and sorted at assessment, since refusing late data would blind the window to the incidents most worth seeing — while events outside the window leave it, so old failures cannot hold a verdict hostage. A perfect window holds its whole budget and burns nothing; an on-target window burns at exactly 1.0. Objectives validate their own shape (ratio in (0,1), positive window and sample, ordered thresholds) and duplicates are refused. Judgment stays in the tracker; the pipeline moves bytes via time-ordered export. **Remaining, and not claimable here:** the metrics pipeline, alerting wiring, and deployed proof.
